# What Else Moved?

## What this article argues

Antoni Gaudí designed the Colònia Güell church using hanging models: cords suspended with weights representing the loads the finished structure would carry, left for gravity to pull into their natural configuration, then inverted to give the structural geometry. The detail that matters is what happens when you move one anchor point — the rest of the model resettles. Forces redistribute, relationships change, and the consequence shows up in the behaviour of the whole structure.

An enterprise introducing AI is also changing loads within a connected system, but usually measures the result as though the point it changed were independent of everything around it. Response times fall 30%. A finance process releases 10,000 hours. Software development accelerates. Each measure may be accurate. But the customer service improvement may increase exception handling elsewhere; the finance automation may increase assurance and review requirements; faster development may increase the volume reaching security, testing and governance. So the question is not only what improved, but **what else moved when it improved**.

Two forms of movement are developed. **Work moves rather than disappearing** — AI removes 1,000 hours from a process while 250 hours appear elsewhere checking outputs, investigating anomalies, tracing evidence and resolving cases the AI cannot safely complete. The original calculation is not wrong; the work has changed form, location and ownership.

**Judgement moves too, across a much longer horizon.** As AI removes or compresses work, it may remove the experiences through which people historically developed judgement. The productivity benefit appears immediately; the capability consequence may take years. Eventually a senior position needs filling, nobody internally is ready, the organisation hires externally at a premium and takes longer to do it. Brad Wolfe's term for the moment the loss finally acquires an economic label is **the hiring invoice**. Productivity up, then years later internal senior capability down, external recruitment up, time to fill up, cost up — plausibly different points on the same organisational pathway rather than separate management problems.

From this the article draws two statements about measurement: the place where value is created may not be the place where cost appears, and the time when the benefit appears may not be the time when the consequence appears.

It closes on a reframing of what the system actually is. The temptation is to treat the technology as the system and the organisation as the environment it is deployed into. The article rejects that: the people reviewing outputs, the experts handling exceptions, the managers exercising judgement, the governance functions setting boundaries, the developmental pathways producing tomorrow's capability, and the reserves of capacity that absorb abnormal conditions are all part of the operating system. AI changes that configuration.

## Where this connects

**Coherence Centric Governance.** This is the clearest statement of what CCG is *for*. Financial, operational and performance measures remain essential; the problem is that they describe individual parts of a system. CCG asks what is happening between those parts as conditions change. The article lists the movements that constitute a configuration change — verification demand, expert workload concentration, escalation behaviour, human participation, authority migration, dependency, recovery capacity, developmental opportunity, proximity to saturation — and adds the question that makes them governable: are we seeing those movements early enough to do something about them?

It also repeats, and sharpens, the positive case introduced in part two. The purpose is not to declare every change harmful. An intervention might improve one domain and strengthen the wider organisation; a productivity gain might create demand the organisation has ample capacity to absorb. Making redistribution visible can support scaling rather than constrain it. The point is that the organisation should know.

**Human Readiness Architecture.** This is where HRA enters the series, and it enters through capability formation rather than individual readiness. The argument that developmental assignments may look automatable until the organisation discovers nobody acquired the judgement they used to produce is the corpus position on why readiness has to be observed over time rather than assumed. The exchange with Ranganath Raghavan puts it plainly: the productivity gain is visible immediately, the loss of the conditions through which judgement develops may stay invisible for years, and by the time the organisation has to import that judgement the recruitment cost is only the visible invoice — the internal pathway that produced the capability has already weakened.

Brad Wolfe proposed tracking the trend in internal fill rates as an imperfect but commercially discussable measure. The reply paired that lagging measure with earlier signals showing whether the experiences through which capability forms still exist. That pairing — a lagging economic indicator alongside leading condition indicators — is the shape HRA measurement takes in enterprise reporting.

**Structural headroom.** The article extends the reserve-capacity argument from part two into human capability: a senior person's apparently underutilised expertise looks expensive until the exceptional case arrives; a review function looks inefficient until AI activity doubles. What looks inefficient locally may be contributing to resilience globally. The valuation problem this creates is raised in the discussion and not resolved until part four.

## Notes for technical readers

The Gaudí analogy is load-bearing and the article's own discussion identifies where it fails. Minas Papagiannopoulos located the boundary precisely: in the hanging model the relationship between a moved anchor and the resettled configuration is physically continuous and directly observable, whereas longitudinal observation of an organisation may show that workload, judgement formation or recovery capacity changed after an AI intervention without establishing that the intervention caused each movement. His caution is that the system view may correct the narrowness of local measurement while quietly acquiring more causal authority than the evidence supports.

The response accepts this as a boundary rather than an objection: "what else moved?" should widen the field of observation, not the causal authority of the evidence. Observation may establish that a change occurred, when, how it developed, and whether it coincided with an intervention — and none of those alone establishes causation. This is the origin of the Detection → Association → Attribution discipline that part four adopts as method.

Ravi Shankar NRK raised a second limit that the article does not answer. If CCG asks what happens between the parts, what is the actual telemetry? How does an enterprise measure dependency increase or judgement erosion before the hiring invoice arrives? Without instrumentation of those movements, CCG risks remaining a post-mortem concept rather than a governing tool. That challenge is the most serious one in the corpus's public discussion to date and is only partly addressed in part four, which names evidence sources without specifying assessment cadence.

The 1,000 hours removed and 250 hours appearing elsewhere are illustrative, not measured.

## Emerging themes

**Maladaptive behaviour as a signal of overload.** Raised by Ravi Shankar NRK, who identified where the Gaudí analogy breaks down structurally rather than evidentially. A hanging model is a closed physical system governed by passive forces — move an anchor and the strings must resettle. Organisations are active and adaptive: when AI redistributes the load of exceptions onto human experts, people do not passively absorb weight until they snap. They invent shadow processes, rubber-stamp AI outputs to clear queues, or leave. So the question is not only what moved, but what maladaptive behaviour the system invented to disguise the fact that it was overloaded. That reframes several RCD indicators as concealment rather than decay, and does not yet have a hub.

**Defensibility of structural headroom against financial optimisation.** Also from Ravi. Spare capacity acting as structural headroom is only protectable if its value can be stated. Where CFOs and activist investors actively hunt inefficiency, capacity that cannot be assigned a defensible figure will be stripped out before the next exception cluster arrives. Part four accepts this as unresolved and suggests reconstruction cost as a direction.

**The telemetry problem.** Recorded above as a limit. It is listed here as well because it is a standing challenge to the framework rather than to this article: until the movements CCG describes can be instrumented in something close to real time, the framework describes what should be observed without establishing that it can be.

Ravi's remaining two questions resolve elsewhere rather than remaining open. The subtractive bias of judgement — whether AI destroys judgement or the organisation fails to redefine the seniority pathway for a new kind of cognitive work — is answered in part four by the reframing from headcount to capability continuity. The limits of human intervention at machine speed is taken up in part four's governance boundary discussion.

Ravi's five questions become the structural spine of part four.
