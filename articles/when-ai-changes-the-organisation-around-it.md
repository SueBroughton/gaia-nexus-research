# When AI Changes the Organisation Around It

**When AI Changes the Organisation Around It**
Published 21 August 2026 · [Read on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/when-ai-changes-organisation-around-sue-broughton-tr5mc/)

## What this article argues

An organisation's AI adoption looks successful by every conventional measure. Decision times are falling, productivity is rising, human overrides are less frequent, escalations are declining, reviews are faster, and employees are increasingly comfortable relying on AI-supported decisions.

The article's opening move is to note that those same indicators could equally accompany a gradual reduction in independent judgement, challenge behaviour or willingness to intervene. **Efficiency and erosion can produce remarkably similar signals.** Declining escalation might mean the AI became more accurate, or that people became more skilled with it, or that processes improved — or that people stopped checking.

From there the argument turns to what the relevant unit of observation should be. Traditional governance is built to detect events: an error, a breached threshold, a failed control, an incident, an investigation. Organisational adaptation to AI may not look like an event at all. There may be no identifiable moment when something went wrong, only hundreds of thousands of individually reasonable decisions — this output looks right, we don't need the second check this time, the system is usually accurate, there's probably no reason to escalate. Each is defensible. The question is what happens when they accumulate over months or years.

That is where the name is introduced: **Enterprise Pathway Drift** — gradual changes in organisational decision pathways, checking behaviours, escalation patterns, challenge practices, trust allocation and human capability that emerge through sustained interaction with AI. The article is careful that drift does not mean deterioration. Some pathways should change, and AI should remove unnecessary work and improve decisions. The challenge is distinguishing healthy adaptation from gradual erosion.

Two further arguments matter for what follows.

**The evidence may already exist.** Escalation histories, override records, review logs, exception records, workflow histories, and survey evidence about trust and willingness to challenge. Individually none of these establishes whether an organisation is becoming more or less capable; together and observed longitudinally they may tell a much richer story. The article's framing is that the missing element is not always more data, but a different question asked of the data already held.

**Visibility is separated from authority.** A junior analyst may notice colleagues no longer questioning AI recommendations; a frontline employee may see a workaround becoming normal practice; a supervisor may notice a review step surviving as a formality. Senior leaders have authority but see aggregated information; frontline workers have direct visibility and limited authority. The governance question is how observations from the edges of the system become legitimate evidence without confusing observation with decision authority — which requires better pathways between observation, evidence, interpretation and authorised human decision-making.

It closes on the reframing the series inherits: some of the most consequential effects of AI adoption do not initially appear as failures. Some appear as improvements.

## Where this connects

**Enterprise Pathway Drift.** This is the naming article and the closest thing to a public specification. The definition given here — decision pathways, checking behaviours, escalation patterns, challenge practices, trust allocation and human capability — maps directly onto the EPD skill range: pathway baseline mapping (97), escalation rate tracking (98), double-check behaviour detection (99), challenge behaviour monitoring (100), trust allocation tracking (101), workaround detection (102). The visibility problem is skill 104, Visibility Without Authority Detection. The healthy-adaptation caution is the drift-versus-erosion distinction the whole skill range is built to hold.

**Coherence Centric Governance.** The evidence sources listed here are the same ones part four of the later series names, stated eight days earlier: escalation histories, override records, review logs, exception records, workflow histories. This article is where they first appear as a set.

**Human Readiness Architecture.** The article distinguishes a capability being formally present from remaining practically available — credentials unchanged, authority unchanged, but the underlying judgement exercised less often. It states the governance consequence directly: a control that depends upon human judgement ultimately depends upon that judgement remaining available. This is the corpus argument for why readiness has to be observed over time rather than assumed from role assignment.

**Relation to the later series.** Several threads start here. The efficiency-versus-erosion problem becomes part two's mirror image. The evidence sources become part four's method. The visibility problem becomes reachability in part four's discussion. And Ricardo Muro's closing question in this thread — if the organisation is adapting around the AI, how do we establish that the controls originally relied upon remain viable in the organisation that now exists — is the question part four answers as continuing reliance, asked eight days before it.

## Notes for technical readers

The article is written in conditional register throughout — may, might, perhaps — and this is deliberate rather than hedging. It proposes a category of effect and a way of looking, not a finding. No measurement, threshold or dataset is offered, and none is implied.

The claim that the evidence may already exist inside operational systems is the article's most testable assertion and its least demonstrated. Escalation histories and override records exist in most organisations; whether they are granular enough, consistent enough over time, or attributable enough to support longitudinal inference is not established here.

The definition of Enterprise Pathway Drift given in this article is broad — six categories of change, any of which might be healthy. That breadth is honest at this stage of the work, but it means the concept as stated does not yet discriminate. The distinguishing test between adaptation and erosion is named as the challenge rather than answered.

## Emerging themes

**Independent reconstruction of judgement as an availability test.** Developed jointly in the discussion by Farah Ahmed and Ricardo Muro, and the most operationally promising idea in this thread. Farah's version comes from production practice: silence is only a good signal if people can still explain the overrides they are *not* making, so periodically ask someone to walk through a case the system handled and defend or challenge the call — if they can, trust is earned; if they cannot, the quiet was atrophy wearing trust's clothes. Ricardo's version generalises it: the harder longitudinal test is not whether people are still in the loop but whether they can independently reconstruct the judgement — identify the relevant evidence, reach a conclusion without inheriting the system's answer, explain what would change that conclusion, and meaningfully challenge the recommendation. The reply added the distinction this is meant to draw: retained understanding versus retrospective agreement.

This is a candidate measurement method for a framework whose stated open problem is measurement, and it does not depend on self-report.

**Intentional and reversible migration of authority.** Raised by Jay VonSpreck. Observation can reveal that authority, judgement or challenge behaviour is migrating, but observation alone does not make that migration governable — the architecture must also establish who has authority to intervene, under what conditions, and while the trajectory can still be changed. His formulation of the test: migration itself is not failure; the governing question is whether the movement is intentional, observable, authorised and reversible, or whether consequential change is occurring outside the enterprise's ability to govern it.

## The discussion

No contributors are acknowledged in the article body. Public discussion following publication:

- **Jay VonSpreck** — fewer interventions may represent healthy adaptation or the migration of authority, judgement, challenge behaviour or intervention capacity, and the number alone cannot say which; the conditions surrounding execution, not merely the technology, as part of the governing object; intervention rights as inseparable from trajectory
- **Farah Ahmed** — the production test for whether silence is earned trust or atrophy
- **Ricardo Muro** — formal presence versus practical availability of a capability; independent reconstruction as the longitudinal test; human oversight as a capability whose state changes over time rather than a static role assignment; and whether controls originally relied upon remain viable in the organisation that now exists

All three appear again in the later series. Farah Ahmed contributes to part one, Ricardo Muro to parts two and four.
