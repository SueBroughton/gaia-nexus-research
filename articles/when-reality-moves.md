# When Reality Moves

**When Reality Moves**

## What this article argues

An AI system is approved under conditions that make the approval reasonable: clear authority, current policy, adequate human review, manageable exception volumes, available expertise, practical intervention, sufficient recovery. Six months later the system is still performing well and no control has failed — but the organisation around it has moved. Review demand has concentrated. Expert utilisation has risen. The provider has changed the model. Oversight staff have moved roles. The system has spread into workflows nobody approved it for.

Nothing failed. The original decision may have been entirely sound. The article asks a different question: **what evidence do we still have that allows us to rely on the conditions under which this system was originally approved?**

The argument turns on a distinction between the decision and the conditions surrounding it. Governance is good at preserving evidence that a decision was justified when it was made — the approval, the policy, the controls, the reasoning. It is much weaker at establishing whether the conditions that made that decision reasonable are still present. Approval is treated as a permanent property of the system rather than a claim about a state of affairs that can lapse.

The article's answer is longitudinal observation: establish what the organisation actually relied upon at the point of decision, identify evidence capable of showing whether those conditions still hold, and watch for movement across several of them together. It closes on a principle stated for the first time here — *if something matters enough for an organisation to rely upon, it matters enough to know whether it is still there* — and on the governance boundary the series has held throughout: the AI observes and reports; the authorised human decides.

## Where this connects

**Coherence Centric Governance.** This is the most operational public statement of CCG so far. Earlier articles argue that drift-centric governance fails one level above the individual control. This one specifies the mechanism: baseline the relied-upon conditions, identify evidence for each, observe movement over time, escalate material change to an authorised person with the evidence attached. It also names the evidence sources — review timestamps, escalation and override records, workflow and exception records, utilisation data, records of workarounds and recovery events, plus structured periodic Human Readiness assessment. That list is the article's most practically useful contribution and marks a shift from diagnosis to method.

**Human Readiness Architecture.** The article makes an explicit correction to a claim that recurs across the corpus. Reduced human participation is *not* evidence of declining capability — a person removed from routine first-level work may be weakening, strengthening, or simply developing differently. The question is reframed from headcount to continuity: are the capabilities the organisation depends upon still being developed and still available as the work changes? This is a meaningful tightening of the HRA position and should be read as superseding looser formulations elsewhere.

**Relational Coherence Debt.** The degradations tracked here — challenge behaviour changing, escalation pathways falling out of use, oversight concentrating among fewer people, review windows compressing, workarounds increasing, trust shifting toward the AI — are RCD indicators stated in enterprise language. The article adds that these need not be read as decay: RCD indicators are treated as signals warranting investigation, not findings.

Not materially connected to the Signature Principle or to BRIDGE and BREAKTHROUGH.

## Notes for technical readers

The article observes a discipline worth noting: **Detection → Association → Attribution**. Observing that expert utilisation rose and challenge behaviour fell after AI deployment does not establish that AI caused either — the organisation may also have restructured, lost staff, or changed incentives. Better visibility is not permission to make stronger causal claims than the evidence supports, and the article accepts that the correct governance output is sometimes only *something important is changing here, we need to understand why*.

Two limits are stated openly rather than papered over.

First, the valuation of structural headroom is unresolved. The article argues that apparently unused capacity — an underutilised expert, a rarely-used escalation pathway, spare review capacity — has value that appears only when it is needed, and concedes that no defensible method yet exists for putting a figure on it. The suggested direction is reconstruction cost: what it would take to rebuild the capability, replace scarce expertise externally, or recreate a developmental pathway after it disappeared. That is a direction, not a method.

Second, cadence is left deliberately open. Some conditions can be evidenced continuously from existing system records; others — human judgement, readiness, governing capability — cannot be reduced to telemetry and require structured periodic assessment. The article does not specify intervals for the second category.

Unlike the cross-pattern article, most of the evidence named here already exists inside the organisation. The claim is not that new instrumentation is required, but that existing records are not currently brought together over time and read as a set.

## Emerging themes

**Reachability of evidence to authority.** Raised by Evelyne-Claudia Yantony in discussion and accepted into the position. Detection is insufficient on its own: a system may correctly identify material change and still be governed by an obsolete warrant if the corrective pathway no longer has causal reach. The question becomes whether evidence of material change can still reach legitimate authority while that authority retains meaningful capacity to alter what happens next. This is a genuine extension of continuing reliance and does not yet have a framework hub.

**Indicator meaning drift.** Raised by Ricardo Muro. The evidence used to observe a condition may remain accurate while what it signifies changes — fewer overrides could indicate better AI performance, declining human challenge, or something else. Longitudinal governance may therefore need to test not only whether the condition persists but whether the indicator still means what it was assumed to mean. This connects back to the representation problem in article two and is the strongest candidate here for later formal treatment.

**Approval conditions as operating requirements.** Raised by Petr Molin. If a deployment was considered safe *because* experienced reviewers had capacity to challenge difficult cases, that capacity is arguably part of the design rather than a condition to be monitored. Monitoring tells you the condition is disappearing; the harder question is whether it should have been protected in the first place. Unresolved in the article.

**Economic valuation of structural headroom.** Named above as a stated limit. Recorded here as a theme because it is the point at which the argument most needs work to survive contact with a CFO.

## The discussion

This article drew the most substantial public exchange in the series to date. Contributors and what each added:

- **Ravi Shankar NRK** — five questions following article three that became the structural spine of this one: organisational maladaptation under load, the change-versus-decline distinction, the valuation of headroom, whether change is actually observable, and the limits of human intervention at machine speed
- **Mo Johnson** — the sentence the article turns on: the decision isn't always what fails, sometimes reality just moves on
- **Minas Papagiannopoulos** — the detection/association/attribution discipline
- **Evelyne-Claudia Yantony** — cumulative materiality, and subsequently reachability
- **Tim Zlomke and Stephen Calhoun** — the distinction between latest-known state and actual-current state
- **Ricardo Muro** — preserving the basis for reliance rather than the original conditions, and indicator meaning drift
- **Petr Molin** — approval assumptions as operating requirements
- **N.G.Z.** — continuous deterministic verification as a sensor layer triggering structured human reassessment
- **Ranganath Raghavan** — initial sign-offs age quickly
- **Charlie Raymond** — historical correctness versus present standing; authority over the next consequence requiring re-establishment
- **HABITS Institute (Heather)** — longitudinal observation and a reality-coupled execution boundary as complementary

The exchange with N.G.Z. produced a formulation worth retaining: continuous evidence where it can be observed, periodic assessment where it cannot, and event-driven human reassessment when material change is detected.
